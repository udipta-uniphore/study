## Part 1: Foundations

### Commutative Rings with Unity
A commutative ring $R$ with unity is a set with addition and multiplication where:
- $(R, +)$ is an abelian group
- Multiplication is commutative and associative
- Multiplication distributes over addition
- There exists a multiplicative identity $1 \in R$

**Units:** An element $u \in R$ is a **unit** if it has a multiplicative inverse—that is, there exists $v \in R$ such that $uv = 1$. The set of all units in $R$ is denoted $R^*$ or $U(R)$.

**Examples:**
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: the units are $\pm 1$ only
- In $\mathbb{Q}$ or any field: every nonzero element is a unit
- In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the units are $\pm 1$ (constant polynomials with inverse in the ring)
- In $F[x]$ (field $F$): every nonzero constant polynomial is a unit

### Integral Domains
An integral domain is a commutative ring with unity where:
- There are no zero divisors: if $ab = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$

### Principal Ideal Domains (PIDs)

A **Principal Ideal Domain** (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is **principal**—that is, every ideal can be written as:
$$(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$$
for a single element $a \in R$.

In other words: no ideal requires multiple generators; one element suffices.

**Examples:**
- $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID—every ideal is $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ for some integer $n$
- $F[x]$ (polynomials over a field $F$) is a PID—every ideal is generated by a single polynomial
- $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ (polynomials over integers) is **not** a PID—the ideal $(2, x)$ generated by both 2 and $x$ cannot be written as $(a)$ for any single polynomial $a$

**Key Properties of PIDs:**

1. **Unique Factorization:** Every nonzero, non-unit element can be written as a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units. This generalizes unique prime factorization from $\mathbb{Z}$.

2. **Irreducible = Prime:** In a PID, an irreducible element $p$ (cannot be factored nontrivially) is automatically prime (if $p | ab$ then $p | a$ or $p | b$).

3. **GCD Exists:** Any two elements have a greatest common divisor, which is principal.

4. **Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules:** A finitely generated module over a PID has a clean decomposition into cyclic modules. This is crucial for understanding tensor constructions over principal domains.

**Why PIDs Matter Here:**

When we work with tensors over a PID (rather than over fields or general commutative rings), the theory becomes much tamer—ideal theory is simpler, factorization is well-behaved, and module structure is tractable. Hoffman-Kunze likely uses PIDs as a key special case.

### Quotient Rings
Given an ideal $I$ in a commutative ring $R$, the **quotient ring** $R/I$ is constructed as follows:
- Elements are **cosets** $r + I = \{r + a : a \in I\}$ for $r \in R$
- Two cosets are equal: $r + I = s + I$ iff $r - s \in I$
- Addition: $(r + I) + (s + I) = (r + s) + I$
- Multiplication: $(r + I)(s + I) = (rs) + I$
- The additive identity is $0 + I = I$ itself

$R/I$ is itself a commutative ring with unity $1 + I$.

**Example:** $\mathbb{Z}/(6) = \{0 + (6), 1 + (6), 2 + (6), 3 + (6), 4 + (6), 5 + (6)\}$. This has 6 elements; arithmetic is done modulo 6.

**Ideals Generated by Multiple Elements:**

The notation $(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n)$ denotes the ideal **generated by** the elements $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n$. It's the smallest ideal containing all of them:
$$(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n) = \{r_1 a_1 + r_2 a_2 + \cdots + r_n a_n : r_i \in R\}$$

These are all finite $R$-linear combinations of the generators.

**Example:** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the ideal $(2, x)$ consists of all polynomials of the form:
$$2p(x) + xq(x)$$
where $p(x), q(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$. This includes $2$, $x$, $2+x$, $2x+x^2$, etc.

**Why $(2, x)$ is not principal:** If $(2, x) = (a)$ for some single polynomial $a$, then both $2$ and $x$ would be divisible by $a$. But in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the only common divisors of $2$ and $x$ are units (constants $\pm 1$), and the ideal generated by a unit is either $\{0\}$ or the whole ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$. So no single polynomial $a$ can generate $(2, x)$—you need both 2 and $x$ as generators.

**Contrast: Why is $(2, x)$ principal in $F[x]$?**

In $F[x]$ (polynomials over a field $F$), the situation is fundamentally different. In a field $F$, every nonzero element is a unit—it has a multiplicative inverse. So the constant polynomial $2$ is invertible in $F[x]$.

Key fact: **Any ideal containing a unit must be the entire ring.** Why? If $u$ is a unit and $u \in I$, then $u^{-1} \in R$ exists, so $1 = u \cdot u^{-1} \in I$, which means $I = R$.

Therefore in $F[x]$: $(2, x) = (1) = F[x]$ (the whole ring). So $(2, x)$ *is* principal—it's generated by 1.

**The key difference:** In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, the element 2 is not a unit. In $F[x]$, every nonzero constant is a unit. This is why ideal structure differs so dramatically between these two rings.


**Cosets in Groups:**

Given a group $(G, *)$ and a subgroup $H \subseteq G$, a **left coset** of $H$ is a set of the form:
$$gH = \{g * h : h \in H\}$$
for some fixed $g \in G$. Similarly, a **right coset** is $Hg = \{h * g : h \in H\}$.

**Key property:** Two cosets $g_1 H$ and $g_2 H$ are either equal or disjoint. They partition $G$ into equivalence classes.

**Example in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$:** The subgroup $H = 6\mathbb{Z}$ (multiples of 6) partitions $\mathbb{Z}$ into 6 cosets:
- $0 + H = \{\ldots, -12, -6, 0, 6, 12, \ldots\}$
- $1 + H = \{\ldots, -11, -5, 1, 7, 13, \ldots\}$
- $2 + H = \{\ldots, -10, -4, 2, 8, 14, \ldots\}$
- ... and so on

**Normal Subgroups:**

A subgroup $N$ of $G$ is **normal** (written $N \triangleleft G$) if left and right cosets coincide:
$$gNg^{-1} = N \quad \text{for all } g \in G$$

Equivalently: $gN = Ng$ for all $g \in G$ (cosets commute with elements).

**Intuition:** A normal subgroup is "stable under conjugation"—if $n \in N$, then $gng^{-1} \in N$ for any $g \in G$.

**Example:** In any group $G$, the center $Z(G)$ (elements that commute with everything) is normal.

**Why Normal Matters:**

If $N$ is normal, you can define a group operation on the cosets $G/N$ by:
$$(g_1 N) * (g_2 N) = (g_1 * g_2) N$$

This is well-defined (doesn't depend on which representative you pick) *only if* $N$ is normal. If $N$ is not normal, this rule breaks down.

**So:** Normal subgroups are exactly the subgroups for which quotient groups exist.

### Field of Fractions

Given an integral domain $R$, the **field of fractions** $\text{Frac}(R)$ is constructed by formally adding multiplicative inverses, similar to how $\mathbb{Q}$ is built from $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Construction:** Elements are pairs $(a, b)$ with $a, b \in R$ and $b \neq 0$, interpreted as the "fraction" $a/b$. Two fractions are equal: $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ iff $ad = bc$.
- Addition: $\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}$
- Multiplication: $\frac{a}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ac}{bd}$

$\text{Frac}(R)$ is a field, and $R$ embeds naturally: $a \mapsto a/1$.

**Examples:**
- $\text{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Q}$
- $\text{Frac}(F[x]) = F(x)$ (rational functions over field $F$)

**Why it matters:** The field of fractions is the "universal" way to embed an integral domain into a field. When studying k-modules, understanding how behavior changes from the field case (where everything is free) to the integral domain case helps motivate why k-modules are more subtle.

### Why k-Modules? Motivation

**Vector spaces** (modules over a field) have a beautiful property: every subspace has a complement, every linear map factors through free modules, and everything is determined by dimension.

**k-modules** (modules over a general commutative ring) lose these guarantees:
- Submodules need not have complements
- Not every module is free (even finitely generated ones)
- Torsion appears: elements $m \neq 0$ with $rm = 0$ for $r \neq 0$

**Why study this?** 
- Tensor constructions over rings are fundamentally more interesting than over fields
- Determinants generalize naturally to k-modules
- Finitely generated modules over PIDs still have nice structure (structure theorem), which bridges the gap
- Applications: algebraic geometry, number theory, and invariant theory all work over rings, not just fields

The theory becomes richer and more subtle—which is exactly why Hoffman-Kunze develop it carefully.

### Complements and Direct Sums

**Complement:** Given a k-module $M$ and a submodule $N \subseteq M$, a **complement** of $N$ is another submodule $N'$ such that:
$$M = N \oplus N'$$

This means:
1. $N \cap N' = \{0\}$ (no overlap except zero)
2. Every element $m \in M$ can be uniquely written as $m = n + n'$ with $n \in N, n' \in N'$

**Example (Vector Spaces):** In $\mathbb{R}^2$, if $N$ = $x$-axis and $N'$ = $y$-axis, then $\mathbb{R}^2 = N \oplus N'$ (direct sum).

**In Vector Spaces (over fields):** Every subspace has a complement.
- **Proof sketch:** Take a basis of $N$, extend it to a basis of $V$. Let $N'$ be spanned by the new basis vectors. Then $V = N \oplus N'$.

**In k-Modules (over rings):** Complements need not exist!

**Counterexample:** In $\mathbb{Z}$ (a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module with basis $\{1\}$), consider the submodule $N = (2) = 2\mathbb{Z}$ (all even integers).

Does $N$ have a complement $N'$ such that $\mathbb{Z} = (2) \oplus N'$?

If such an $N'$ existed, then:
- Every integer would split uniquely as $2a + n'$ with $2a \in (2)$ and $n' \in N'$
- In particular, $1 = 2a + n'$ for some $a, n'$
- But if $n' \in N'$ and $1 = 2a + n'$, then $n'$ is odd
- Similarly, $3 = 2(1) + n' = 2 + n'$ forces $n' = 1$ here too
- So both 1 and 3 must be in $N'$, but then $1 - 3 = -2 \in N'$
- This means $(2) \cap N' \neq \{0\}$ (since $-2 \in (2)$ and also in $N'$)

Contradiction! So no complement exists for $(2)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Why this matters:** Over fields, you can always "factor out" a subspace and work with the complement. Over rings, you can't always do this. This makes k-module theory fundamentally different and more intricate.



### Free Modules

A k-module $M$ is **free** if it has a basis—a subset $\{e_i\}_{i \in I}$ such that every element of $M$ can be uniquely written as a finite sum:
$$m = \sum_{i \in I} r_i e_i$$
where $r_i \in k$ and only finitely many are nonzero.

**Examples:**
- $k^n$ (n-tuples) is a free k-module of rank $n$
- The ideal $(2, x) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is NOT free (not every finitely generated module is free)
- Every vector space is a free module (over its field)

**Key property:** Over a PID, every finitely generated torsion-free module is free. This is powerful.

**Rank:** For a free k-module $M$, the rank is the cardinality of a basis. Over an integral domain (and more generally over commutative rings), rank is well-defined.

### Module Homomorphisms

A **k-module homomorphism** (or k-linear map) $f: M \to N$ between two k-modules is a function satisfying:
$$f(m_1 + m_2) = f(m_1) + f(m_2), \quad f(rm) = rf(m) \quad \text{for all } r \in k, m_1, m_2 \in M$$

**Examples:**
- Multiplication by $r \in k$: the map $M \to M$ sending $m \mapsto rm$
- The determinant (once defined): a multilinear map from $M^n$ to $k$

**Key facts:**
- The set of k-module homomorphisms $\text{Hom}_k(M, N)$ forms a k-module itself (with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication)
- The kernel and image of a homomorphism are submodules
- First isomorphism theorem: $M/\ker(f) \cong \text{im}(f)$

**Why it matters for tensors:** Multilinear maps are collections of module homomorphisms in each variable separately. Understanding single homomorphisms is the stepping stone.

**Quotient Group vs. Quotient Ring:**

Both quotient groups and quotient rings use the same fundamental coset machinery:
- **Quotient Group** $G/H$: Takes cosets of a normal subgroup $H$ in a group $G$. Operations are inherited from $G$.
- **Quotient Ring** $R/I$: Takes cosets of an ideal $I$ in a ring $R$. Both addition and multiplication are inherited and respect the quotient structure.

In fact, every quotient ring is partially a quotient group—when you form $R/I$, you're quotienting the additive group $(R, +)$ by the ideal $I$ (viewed as an additive subgroup). The extra ingredient is that multiplication also closes under the quotient.

| Aspect | Quotient Group | Quotient Ring |
|--------|---|---|
| Start with | Group $(G, *)$ | Ring $(R, +, \cdot)$ |
| Quotient by | Normal subgroup $H$ | Ideal $I$ |
| Elements of quotient | Cosets $gH$ | Cosets $r + I$ |
| Inherited operation(s) | One: $*$ | Two: $+$ and $\cdot$ |
| Key requirement | $H$ must be normal | $I$ absorbs multiplication on both sides |

**Quotient Topology: A Different Beast**

Quotient topology is fundamentally different—it's a *topological* rather than *algebraic* construction:
- **Quotient Topology** on $X/\sim$: Given a space $X$ and equivalence relation $\sim$, declare a set $U$ open in the quotient iff its preimage in $X$ is open.
- This is about inheritance of open sets, not about operations.

**Key Difference:** 
- Quotient group/ring inherit *operations* that make them algebraic structures
- Quotient topology inherits a *notion of openness* that makes it a topological space

They live in different categories. However, they can coexist—if you have both algebraic operations and a topology (like quotient manifolds), you may use both quotient constructions simultaneously.

### Prime Ideals
A prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ in a commutative ring $R$ is an ideal where:
- $\mathfrak{p} \neq R$
- If $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$, then $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$

**Key fact:** $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain.

### Maximal Ideals
A maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ in a commutative ring $R$ is an ideal satisfying:
$$\mathfrak{m} \neq R \quad \text{and} \quad \text{if } \mathfrak{m} \subseteq I \subseteq R \text{ for an ideal } I, \text{ then } I = \mathfrak{m} \text{ or } I = R$$

In other words: $\mathfrak{m}$ is the largest proper ideal.

**Examples:**
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: $(p) = p\mathbb{Z}$ for prime $p$ is maximal
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: $(4) = 4\mathbb{Z}$ is NOT maximal (since $(4) \subsetneq (2) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$)
- In a field: only $\{0\}$ is maximal

**Key properties:**
1. $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field
2. Every maximal ideal is prime
3. Every proper ideal is contained in some maximal ideal

**Maximal vs. Prime:**
- Every maximal ideal is prime, but not vice versa
- Example: $(0)$ in an integral domain is prime but rarely maximal

## Part 2: k-Modules

### Definition of a k-Module

Let $k$ be a commutative ring with unity. A **k-module** (or module over $k$) is an abelian group $(M, +)$ together with a scalar multiplication operation $\cdot: k \times M \to M$ satisfying:

1. **Distributivity over addition in $M$:** $r(m_1 + m_2) = rm_1 + rm_2$ for all $r \in k, m_1, m_2 \in M$
2. **Distributivity over addition in $k$:** $(r_1 + r_2)m = r_1 m + r_2 m$ for all $r_1, r_2 \in k, m \in M$
3. **Compatibility with multiplication in $k$:** $r_1(r_2 m) = (r_1 r_2)m$ for all $r_1, r_2 \in k, m \in M$
4. **Unitality:** $1 \cdot m = m$ for all $m \in M$ (where $1$ is the multiplicative identity of $k$)

In other words: a k-module is a generalization of a vector space where the "scalars" come from a ring rather than a field.

### Relationship to Other Structures

**When $k$ is a field:** A k-module is exactly a vector space over $k$.

**When $k = \mathbb{Z}$:** A $\mathbb{Z}$-module is exactly an abelian group. (Scalar multiplication by $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ is repeated addition.)

**More generally:** If you've seen abelian groups, vector spaces, or both—k-modules unify and generalize them.

### Examples of k-Modules

**Example 1:** $k^n$ (the $n$-fold Cartesian product of $k$ with itself) is a k-module under componentwise addition and scalar multiplication. This is always free with basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$.

**Example 2:** Let $k = \mathbb{Z}$ and $M = \mathbb{Z}$. Then $\mathbb{Z}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module (the additive group of integers). The submodule $(2) = 2\mathbb{Z}$ is the ideal of even integers, which we saw doesn't have a complement.

**Example 3:** Let $k = \mathbb{Z}$ and $M = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (integers mod $n$). This is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. For instance, $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has 6 elements and torsion (e.g., $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ even though $2, 3 \neq 0$).

**Example 4:** Let $k = F[x]$ (polynomials over a field $F$) and $M = k$ itself. Then $k$ is a k-module under ordinary addition and polynomial multiplication. More interestingly, quotient modules like $F[x]/(x^2 - 1)$ are also modules.

### Submodules

A **submodule** $N$ of a k-module $M$ is a subset $N \subseteq M$ that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication:
- If $m_1, m_2 \in N$, then $m_1 + m_2 \in N$
- If $r \in k$ and $m \in N$, then $rm \in N$

This is the k-module analogue of a subspace or subgroup.

**Examples:**
- In $\mathbb{Z}$ (as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), the submodules are $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$—the ideals
- In any k-module $M$, the ideals $I \subseteq k$ give submodules: $IM = \{im : i \in I, m \in M\}$

### Quotient Modules

If $N$ is a submodule of $M$, the **quotient module** $M/N$ is the abelian group of cosets $m + N$ with scalar multiplication defined by:
$$r(m + N) = (rm) + N$$

This is well-defined (independent of representative choice) precisely because $N$ is a submodule.

**First Isomorphism Theorem for Modules:** If $f: M \to M'$ is a k-module homomorphism, then:
$$M/\ker(f) \cong \text{im}(f)$$

**Proof:**

Define a map $\bar{f}: M/\ker(f) \to \text{im}(f)$ by:
$$\bar{f}(m + \ker(f)) = f(m)$$

*Step 1: Well-defined.* If $m_1 + \ker(f) = m_2 + \ker(f)$, then $m_1 - m_2 \in \ker(f)$, so $f(m_1) = f(m_2)$. Thus $\bar{f}$ is well-defined.

*Step 2: k-module homomorphism.* 
- $\bar{f}((m_1 + \ker(f)) + (m_2 + \ker(f))) = \bar{f}((m_1 + m_2) + \ker(f)) = f(m_1 + m_2) = f(m_1) + f(m_2) = \bar{f}(m_1 + \ker(f)) + \bar{f}(m_2 + \ker(f))$
- $\bar{f}(r(m + \ker(f))) = \bar{f}(rm + \ker(f)) = f(rm) = rf(m) = r\bar{f}(m + \ker(f))$

*Step 3: Bijective.*
- **Surjective:** For any $y \in \text{im}(f)$, we have $y = f(m)$ for some $m \in M$. Then $\bar{f}(m + \ker(f)) = f(m) = y$.
- **Injective:** If $\bar{f}(m + \ker(f)) = 0$, then $f(m) = 0$, so $m \in \ker(f)$, hence $m + \ker(f) = 0 + \ker(f)$ in $M/\ker(f)$.

Therefore $\bar{f}$ is an isomorphism of k-modules, completing the proof. $\square$

### Torsion Elements

An element $m \in M$ is a **torsion element** if there exists a nonzero $r \in k$ such that $rm = 0$.

The **torsion submodule** $T(M)$ is the set of all torsion elements—it's a submodule.

**Examples:**
- In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ (as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), every element is torsion (e.g., $6 \cdot (any element) = 0$)
- In $\mathbb{Z}$ (as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), the only torsion element is $0$ (since $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free)
- In $\mathbb{Z}/(2) \times \mathbb{Z}$ (as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), elements like $(1, 0)$ are torsion while elements like $(0, 1)$ are not

**Why torsion matters:** Torsion elements appear only in modules over non-fields. They don't exist in vector spaces but are central to understanding modules over rings like $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

### Free Modules Revisited

A k-module $M$ is **free** if it has a basis—a subset $\{e_i\}_{i \in I}$ such that every element of $M$ is a unique finite sum:
$$m = \sum_{i \in I} r_i e_i \quad (r_i \in k, \text{ finitely many nonzero})$$

**Key observation:** Over a field $k$, every module is free (every vector space has a basis). Over a general ring $k$, not every module is free.

**Rank of a free module:** If $M$ is a free k-module, its rank is the cardinality of a basis. Over an integral domain (or more generally), rank is well-defined.

**Examples:**
- $k^n$ is free of rank $n$
- $\mathbb{Z}$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank 1
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is NOT free as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module (it's finite but has torsion)



## Part 3: Multilinear Maps & Tensors

### Bilinear Maps

A **bilinear map** is a function $f: M_1 \times M_2 \to N$ (where $M_1, M_2, N$ are k-modules) satisfying:

**Linearity in the first argument:**
$$f(r_1 m_1 + r_2 m_1', m_2) = r_1 f(m_1, m_2) + r_2 f(m_1', m_2)$$

**Linearity in the second argument:**
$$f(m_1, r_1 m_2 + r_2 m_2') = r_1 f(m_1, m_2) + r_2 f(m_1, m_2')$$

In other words: $f$ is linear in each variable when the other is held fixed.

**Examples:**

1. **Dot product:** $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $(u, v) \mapsto u \cdot v$ is bilinear
2. **Multiplication in a ring:** $R \times R \to R$ given by $(r, s) \mapsto rs$ is bilinear (when viewing $R$ as an $R$-module)
3. **Tensor contraction:** If $M$ and $N$ are k-modules, any element of $\text{Hom}_k(M, N) \times M$ can be evaluated: $(f, m) \mapsto f(m)$ is bilinear
4. **Matrix multiplication:** The map $M_{m \times n} \times M_{n \times p} \to M_{m \times p}$ sending $(A, B) \mapsto AB$ is bilinear

### General Multilinear Maps

A **multilinear map** (or $n$-linear map) is a function $f: M_1 \times M_2 \times \cdots \times M_n \to N$ that is linear in each argument separately:
$$f(m_1, \ldots, r_i m_i + r_i' m_i', \ldots, m_n) = r_i f(m_1, \ldots, m_i, \ldots, m_n) + r_i' f(m_1, \ldots, m_i', \ldots, m_n)$$
for each $i = 1, \ldots, n$.

**Examples:**

1. **Determinant:** $\det: M_n(k) \to k$ is an $n$-linear map in the rows (or columns) when $k$ is a commutative ring
2. **Scalar triple product:** $(u, v, w) \mapsto u \cdot (v \times w)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is 3-linear
3. **Polynomial evaluation:** Fix $k_1, \ldots, k_n \in k$. The map $M^n \to M$ sending $(m_1, \ldots, m_n) \mapsto k_1 m_1 + \cdots + k_n m_n$ is $n$-linear

### The Space of Multilinear Maps

The set of all k-bilinear maps $M_1 \times M_2 \to N$ forms a k-module under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication:
$$(f + g)(m_1, m_2) = f(m_1, m_2) + g(m_1, m_2)$$
$$(rf)(m_1, m_2) = r \cdot f(m_1, m_2)$$

This is denoted $\text{Bil}_k(M_1, M_2; N)$. Similarly, $\text{Mult}_k(M_1, \ldots, M_n; N)$ denotes $n$-linear maps.

### Alternating Multilinear Maps

An $n$-linear map $f: M^n \to N$ is **alternating** if:
$$f(m_1, \ldots, m_i, \ldots, m_j, \ldots, m_n) = 0 \quad \text{whenever } m_i = m_j \text{ for some } i \neq j$$

Equivalently (in many settings), swapping two arguments changes the sign:
$$f(\ldots, m_i, \ldots, m_j, \ldots) = -f(\ldots, m_j, \ldots, m_i, \ldots)$$

**Examples:**

1. **Determinant:** The determinant is alternating (swap two rows, determinant changes sign)
2. **Cross product:** In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the map $(u, v) \mapsto u \times v$ is alternating 2-linear
3. **Wedge product:** $\Lambda^n(M)$ (to be defined) is built from alternating multilinear maps

### The Alternation Operator

There is a natural **projection** from multilinear maps to alternating multilinear maps, called the **alternation operator**.

Given an $n$-linear map $f: M^n \to N$, define:
$$\text{Alt}(f)(m_1, \ldots, m_n) = \frac{1}{n!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \text{sgn}(\sigma) \cdot f(m_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, m_{\sigma(n)})$$

where $S_n$ is the symmetric group on $n$ elements and $\text{sgn}(\sigma)$ is the sign of the permutation $\sigma$ (i.e., $(-1)^{\text{\# of transpositions}}$).

**Properties:**

1. **$\text{Alt}(f)$ is alternating:** By construction, swapping two arguments introduces a sign change, and the sum over all permutations symmetrizes this out to zero when any two arguments are equal (when $n!$ is invertible in $k$).

2. **Idempotent on alternating maps:** If $f$ is already alternating, then $\text{Alt}(f) = f$.

3. **Functorial:** $\text{Alt}$ respects the module structure: $\text{Alt}(f + g) = \text{Alt}(f) + \text{Alt}(g)$ and $\text{Alt}(rf) = r \cdot \text{Alt}(f)$.

**Example:** For a bilinear map $f: M \times M \to N$,
$$\text{Alt}(f)(m_1, m_2) = \frac{1}{2}[f(m_1, m_2) - f(m_2, m_1)]$$

This is precisely the antisymmetric part of $f$.

**Caveat:** The alternation operator is well-defined and idempotent when $n!$ is invertible in $k$ (true for fields or over $\mathbb{Z}$ when $n!$ divides into the coefficient). Over general rings, the factor $\frac{1}{n!}$ requires care.

### Exterior Algebra (Grassmann Algebra)

The **exterior algebra** $\Lambda(M)$ of a k-module $M$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra by the ideal generated by elements of the form $m \otimes m$ (for $m \in M$). More precisely:

$$\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty} \Lambda^n(M)$$

where $\Lambda^0(M) = k$ and $\Lambda^n(M)$ is the quotient of $M \otimes M \otimes \cdots \otimes M$ (n times) by the relations $m_i \otimes m_j = 0$ whenever $m_i = m_j$.

**The Wedge Product:**

Elements of $\Lambda^n(M)$ are written using the **wedge product** $\wedge$:
$$m_1 \wedge m_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_n$$

The wedge product satisfies:
1. **Anti-commutativity:** $m_1 \wedge m_2 = -m_2 \wedge m_1$ (swapping two factors introduces a sign)
2. **Nilpotency:** $m \wedge m = 0$ (any repeated factor vanishes)
3. **Multilinearity:** $(r_1 m_1 + r_2 m_1') \wedge m_2 = r_1(m_1 \wedge m_2) + r_2(m_1' \wedge m_2)$

**Connection to Alternating Maps:**

An element $\omega \in \Lambda^n(M)$ corresponds to an alternating $n$-linear map $f: M^n \to k$ via:
$$\omega(m_1, \ldots, m_n) := f(m_1, \ldots, m_n) \text{ for } \omega = m_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge m_n$$

More generally, $\Lambda^n(M)$ **represents** alternating $n$-linear maps: there is a natural isomorphism
$$\text{Hom}_k(\Lambda^n(M), N) \cong \text{Alt}_k^n(M; N)$$
between k-module homomorphisms from $\Lambda^n(M)$ and alternating $n$-linear maps from $M^n$.

**Examples:**

1. **$\Lambda^1(M) = M$** — the first exterior power is just $M$ itself
2. **$\Lambda^2(M)$** — for $M = k^n$, this is the space of all 2-forms (antisymmetric bilinear maps), with dimension $\binom{n}{2}$
3. **$\Lambda^n(k^n)$** — one-dimensional, generated by $e_1 \wedge e_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n$. Any element is a scalar multiple of this basis element.

**Why Grassmann Algebras Matter:**

- **Determinants live here:** The determinant is precisely the element of $\Lambda^n(k^n)$ that evaluates multilinearly on the rows (or columns) of a matrix
- **Differential geometry:** Differential forms are sections of exterior algebras of the cotangent bundle
- **Algebraic topology:** Exterior algebras compute cohomology rings
- **Invariant theory:** The algebra structure of $\Lambda(M)$ encodes geometric invariants

The wedge product gives $\Lambda(M)$ a **graded algebra structure**—you can multiply elements: 
$$(m_1 \wedge m_2) \wedge (m_3 \wedge m_4) = m_1 \wedge m_2 \wedge m_3 \wedge m_4$$
with the convention that swapping across a "wedge boundary" introduces signs carefully (super-commutativity).


The tensor product $M_1 \otimes_k M_2 \otimes_k \cdots \otimes_k M_n$ is defined universally by:

**Universal Property:** For any $n$-linear map $f: M_1 \times \cdots \times M_n \to N$, there exists a **unique** k-module homomorphism $\bar{f}: M_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes M_n \to N$ such that:
$$f(m_1, \ldots, m_n) = \bar{f}(m_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes m_n)$$

In other words: **multilinear maps into a module are in bijection with linear maps out of the tensor product**.

This is the fundamental reason tensors exist—they linearize multilinearity.
